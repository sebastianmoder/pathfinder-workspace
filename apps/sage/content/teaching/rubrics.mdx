# Using Generative AI to Create Effective Assessment Rubrics

<Callout type="warning">
  Build the rubric from approved outcomes and the assignment—not from student
  submissions. Do not upload identifiable work to an unapproved tool, and do
  not let the model determine a grade.
</Callout>

## What AI can contribute

An analytic rubric connects criteria with descriptions of observable performance
at different levels. Rubrics can clarify expectations and support consistent
grading and formative feedback when they are aligned, understood, and tested;
their appearance alone does not establish validity or reliability.[^JonssonSvingby2007Rubrics]
[^PanaderoJonsson2013Rubrics]

AI can propose wording and formats from supplied outcomes, assignment
instructions, and standards. It cannot establish that the criteria are fair or
that two graders will interpret a descriptor in the same way. Use it to draft
alternatives, then test the rubric with people.

## Draft, calibrate, and publish

1. **Define the evidence.** Identify the capability each criterion measures and
   what the submitted work can actually show. Do not add language or presentation
   criteria unless they belong to the outcomes.
2. **Draft the levels.** Supply required criteria, number of levels, and any
   agreed weights. Ask for observable differences; replace labels such as
   “excellent insight” with evidence another grader can locate.
3. **Check overlap and fairness.** Remove double-counting and requirements absent
   from the brief. Inspect cultural and linguistic assumptions that could
   disadvantage students.[^Ntoutsi2020Bias]
4. **Calibrate with colleagues.** Independently apply the draft to several
   educator-created or appropriately authorised anchor responses. Compare the
   evidence and level selected, resolve disagreements, and revise descriptors.
   Synthetic anchors are useful for drafting but do not establish reliability
   across real student work.
5. **Share before use.** Publish the reviewed rubric and discuss examples with
   students. Keep the announced criteria stable during assessment; handle any
   necessary correction through institutional procedures.

## Copyable rubric-drafting prompt

```text
Draft an analytic rubric using only the supplied assignment, learning outcomes,
and required standards.

For each criterion:
- map it to one or more learning outcomes;
- describe observable evidence rather than personality, effort, or intent;
- write four performance levels with differences that another qualified grader
  could distinguish; and
- flag overlap, missing evidence, or requirements unsupported by the assignment.

Return:
1. an outcome-to-criterion mapping;
2. the draft rubric table;
3. questions the educator must resolve; and
4. a checklist for testing the rubric on synthetic anchor responses.

Do not add presentation, language, citation, or formatting criteria unless the
supplied outcomes or assignment require them. Do not assign weights when their
relative importance has not been provided.

[assignment]
[learning outcomes]
[required standards]
```

## Example: distinguish adjacent levels

For a criterion about evaluating evidence, “good use of sources” and “excellent
use of sources” leave the difference unclear. A more checkable distinction might
be “uses relevant evidence but does not address a material conflicting finding”
and “weighs relevant and conflicting evidence and explains how it affects the
conclusion”. Adapt this to the taught outcome and test it on anchor responses;
these are illustrative descriptors, not a validated universal scale.

Keep the educator responsible for applying the rubric and deciding the grade.
For feedback drafting, use [Providing feedback](/teaching/feedback); for a pilot
record covering quality and review time, use [Evaluating an AI teaching
workflow](/teaching/evaluating_ai).

## Worked calibration: resolve a disagreement

Use the [synthetic submission and evidence pack](/teaching/feedback#worked-review-correct-a-misleading-feedback-draft)
with two colleagues. The draft evidence criterion says only “uses sources
effectively”. Ask each colleague to select a level and locate the evidence
before comparing judgments.

| Grader | Initial judgment | What the judgment reveals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | High: the paragraph cites a survey, gives numbers, and links them to a recommendation | “Effective” has been interpreted as including evidence and using it persuasively |
| B | Low: the paragraph generalises from self-selected respondents and claims an unmeasured learning benefit | “Effective” has been interpreted as making warranted inferences |

These are illustrative judgments, not measured reliability results. The team
should return to the learning outcome: evaluating what evidence supports. On
that outcome, citing a number is insufficient when the conclusion exceeds it.

Replace the vague descriptor with observable distinctions: **0**, makes a material
inference the evidence does not support; **1**, interprets the central evidence
accurately but leaves a material limitation unexplained; **2**, interprets it
accurately and explains how the limitation affects the recommendation. The
original excerpt falls at 0 for this criterion because of its unsupported
population and causal claims; a correct opening sentence does not resolve them.

Agree how to handle mixed evidence, then independently apply the revised
descriptor to additional anchor responses, including a well-supported conclusion
different from the educator's preference. Record remaining disagreements and
revise before publication. Do not change an announced rubric informally during
marking. This small exercise improves the specificity of the draft; it does not
establish reliability for a whole cohort.
