# AI-Assisted Data Analysis

AI can propose an analysis plan, explain a formula, draft code, or critique a
chart. Students still need to decide what the data can answer and reproduce the
result. A working script or polished chart is insufficient evidence of a sound
analysis.

The American Statistical Association's ethical guidance emphasises data
provenance, transparent assumptions, reproducible results, and valid
interpretation.[^ASA] It provides professional principles for this guide, not
evidence that the proposed AI activity improves learning.

<Callout type="warning">
  Start with synthetic or openly licensed teaching data. Real records may
  contain personal, commercially sensitive, or research-confidential information.
  Check the tool and data under [Responsible AI use](/genai/responsible_use).
  Do not run generated code with access to protected files, credentials, or
  services merely to see whether it works.
</Callout>

## Preserve the analytical decisions

Before using AI, ask students to write the question, unit of observation,
quantity to estimate, denominator, and a prediction they can justify. Decide
which capabilities the [assignment](/teaching/course_ai_policy) requires them
to perform independently.

| Stage | Possible AI assistance | Student or educator verification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Define the question | Suggest alternative formulations | Identify what the data actually measure and which claims are outside scope |
| Inspect the data | Propose checks for types, missingness, duplicates, or unusual values | Compare with the data dictionary and inspect records |
| Clean and combine | Draft transformations or joins | Record before/after counts, keys, dropped rows, and decisions about missing values |
| Calculate or model | Explain an approved method or draft implementation | Check assumptions, hand-calculate a small case, and rerun the analysis |
| Visualise | Propose a chart or labels | Verify scales, denominators, uncertainty, accessible presentation, and correspondence with the table |
| Interpret | Identify possible alternative explanations | Separate association from causation and state the limits of the design |

Do not select a statistical test because the model recommends it or repeatedly
change methods until an appealing result appears. Use the methods taught in the
course, document changes, and seek qualified guidance when the analysis exceeds
the student's or educator's expertise.

## Worked exercise: a misleading conversion rate

Everything in this exercise is synthetic. It represents counts of website
visits and purchases, not people or actual business results. A conversion here
means a visit resulting in one purchase; assume at most one purchase per visit.

```csv
channel,visits,purchases
A,90,9
B,10,9
```

**Question:** what proportion of all recorded visits resulted in a purchase?

Give learners this intentionally flawed AI-style report:

> Channel A converts at 10% and B at 90%. The overall conversion rate is
> therefore 50%. B's advertising caused more customers to buy, so all future
> spending should move to B.

The report is educator-authored for critique; no live model is needed. Ask
students to mark each calculation, inference, and recommendation separately.

### Educator answer key

The two channel rates are 9 / 90 = 10% and 9 / 10 = 90%. Their simple mean is
50%, but that is not the pooled proportion of purchases across visits. There
are 18 purchases and 100 visits, giving **18%**. The weighted calculation gives
the same result: (90 × 10% + 10 × 90%) / 100 = 18%.

Channel B has the higher observed rate, but the table contains no random
assignment, spending, customer characteristics, or repeated periods. It does
not establish a causal advertising effect or support moving all spending.
The small B denominator also makes a single additional purchase a much larger
change in its rate than in A's. Students should identify the additional
evidence needed rather than invent it.

Use a labelled table alongside any chart. Inspect whether a truncated axis or
unlabelled percentage exaggerates the comparison. Keep count data available to
readers who cannot use the visual.

### A second fault: duplicate rows after a join

Suppose a channel-description table contains A once and B twice. Joining it to
the dataset on channel would duplicate B's counts. A naive aggregation then
reports 27 purchases / 110 visits, about 24.5%, rather than 18%.

Ask students to explain the expected relationship between tables, check key
uniqueness, and compare row counts and totals before and after the join. Resolve
which description is valid; silently dropping a duplicate is not a substitute
for understanding its origin. This check applies whether students use code,
spreadsheets, or a graphical analysis tool.

## A prompt that requests checks

```text
Help review my analysis plan; do not choose a method or claim a result for me.

Question: [question and quantity to estimate]
Data dictionary and permitted synthetic sample: [material]
Methods taught and allowed: [methods]
My plan and first calculation: [attempt]

Identify assumptions, denominator choices, missing-data decisions, and join
conditions that could change the answer. For each, suggest a check I can carry
out independently. Flag questions the data cannot answer. Distinguish code you
have actually executed from code you are proposing. Do not invent observations,
results, references, or statistical significance.
```

If execution is available, inspect the code and environment before running it.
Save the input version, transformations, formulas or script, relevant software
versions, and output in approved storage. Reproduce the result from the original
input, rather than accepting numbers copied from a chat.

## Assess reasoning and transfer

Ask for a short audit containing the question, data provenance, a reproducible
calculation, one consequential check, and a justified interpretation. A selected
decision record is usually more useful than a complete conversation.

Use a fresh dataset to assess denominator choice and explanation without AI,
under the announced conditions and accommodations. The [quantitative assessment
example](/teaching/assessment_examples#example-2-a-quantitative-analysis-audit)
provides a brief and rubric. For implementation skills, see [Teaching programming
with AI](/students/programming); for evaluating claims and sources, use
[Evaluating AI outputs](/students/ai_literacy).
