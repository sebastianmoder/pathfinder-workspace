# Teaching Students to Critically Evaluate AI Outputs

Students need a repeatable way to inspect claims, sources, omissions, and
framing. This guide helps educators design activities that make those judgments
visible. For the underlying risks, use [Limitations and risks](/genai/limitations);
for the wider curriculum pathway, use [Building AI competencies](/students/ai_competencies).

## Prepare an artefact students can check

Choose a course-relevant output, a source pack students can access, and a
specific evaluation goal. Inspect the output in advance and retain an answer
key that distinguishes accurate, unsupported, misleading, and missing content.
Include good claims as well as weaknesses so the task rewards judgment rather
than automatic rejection.

Fluent presentation does not establish accuracy. Models can invent citations,
misrepresent real sources, or omit competing perspectives; even a
retrieval-enabled product can link a claim to the wrong passage.[^Huang2024Survey]
[^AlkaissiMcFarlane2023Hallucinations] Bias may appear in framing and omissions as
well as explicit stereotypes.[^Ntoutsi2020Bias]

Supply a shared artefact when individual accounts are unnecessary. This gives
students an equivalent starting point without subscriptions, uploads, or live
model variability. Provide accessible formats and instructions in advance.

## A Framework for Evaluation

Rather than asking students to "check if the AI is right" (which offers no method), give them a structured lens. The four questions below can be applied to any AI output, across any subject.

### 1. Is it accurate?

Ask students to identify checkable claims and verify a manageable sample
against authoritative sources. Sampling is a teaching exercise, not validation
of the entire output. For work that will be submitted, distributed, or used in a
consequential decision, check every material claim and reference. Depending on
the discipline, use primary records, peer-reviewed research, official data, or
approved reference texts. Mark unresolved claims as unresolved.

### 2. Is it complete?

Ask students to identify what the AI left out. This is often more revealing than catching errors. What counterarguments were not mentioned? Which groups, perspectives, or complicating cases were absent? What does the omission imply about the limits of using this tool for this kind of question?

### 3. Is it appropriately uncertain?

Ask students to compare the AI's expressed confidence to the actual state of knowledge in the field. In areas of genuine academic debate, does the AI present one view as settled fact? Does it use hedging language where the evidence warrants it? This question trains students to read claims critically rather than passively.

### 4. Whose perspective does it reflect?

Ask students to consider whose knowledge and whose framing is present in the response, and whose is not. This is especially productive in the humanities and social sciences, but applies anywhere. A response about economic development policy will embed assumptions; a response about a historical conflict will centre some actors and marginalise others.

## Classroom Activities

The activities below are designed to make evaluation visible, discussable, and disciplinary. Each can be scaled from a short in-class exercise to a more extended assignment.

### The Fact-Check Sprint

Give students an AI-generated explanation of a concept from your course. Their task is to fact-check three specific claims within a set time limit, using sources you approve. Afterward, discuss as a group: Which claims were accurate? Which were wrong or misleading? Which were impossible to verify? This exercise works well at the start of a unit to prime students' scepticism before they engage with the topic independently.

### Source Verification

Provide an educator-vetted set of citations from an AI output, including any
errors you have already identified. Students check the bibliographic record,
open each source, locate the supporting passage, and explain whether it supports
the associated claim. Distinguish “does not exist”, “exists but does not support
this claim”, and “could not access or verify”. Do not require students to
generate a bibliography and then assume it contains suitable errors.

### Side-by-Side Comparison

Provide students with two explanations of the same concept: one AI-generated, one from a course-approved source such as a textbook chapter, journal article, or expert commentary. Ask students to annotate both: What does each cover that the other does not? Where do they agree and disagree? What would someone who read only the AI version believe that is incomplete or incorrect? This works particularly well for contested topics where the human-authored source expresses reasoned uncertainty that the AI flattens.

### The Omission Exercise

Rather than asking what the AI got wrong, ask students to identify what a good answer to this question would need to include, before they see the AI response. Then compare their list against what the AI actually produced. <mark>Asking students to construct evaluation criteria before seeing the output</mark> can reduce reliance on the AI’s framing and makes students’ disciplinary
criteria explicit.

### Perspective Audit

Give students an AI-generated response on a topic that involves multiple stakeholders, communities, or schools of thought — a policy question, an ethical dilemma, a historical event. Ask them to map which perspectives are present, which are absent, and how the response might read differently if it had been generated from a different starting position. This is especially generative in law, social sciences, ethics, and history.

### Multimodal Provenance Check

Use an AI-generated or AI-edited image, chart, audio clip, video, or code sample.
Ask students to identify what can be established from the artefact itself, what
requires provenance or source information, and which details must be checked
against primary material. Include accessibility: provide alt text, transcripts,
or an equivalent format and ask whether the generated representation introduces
misleading or exclusionary choices.

### Rewrite the Response

After identifying weaknesses in an AI output, ask students to revise it. This could mean correcting factual errors, adding missing nuance, incorporating sources, or restructuring the argument. The revision task makes evaluation productive rather than merely critical and demonstrates to students where their knowledge exceeds the AI's. Requiring students to annotate their changes — explaining what they corrected and why — produces strong evidence of learning for [assessment](/students/exams/) purposes.

## Discipline-Specific Considerations

The same framework applies across subjects, but the most productive activities vary by discipline.

In **STEM fields**, the highest-value target is numerical claims, equations, and procedural steps. AI models often produce plausible but subtly wrong derivations or incorrectly simplified formulas. Ask students to work through AI-generated solutions independently and identify any step that could not be reproduced from first principles.

In **the humanities**, focus on interpretation and attribution. Ask students to check whether the AI's reading of a text is defensible, and whether it accurately characterises the views of scholars it appears to cite. A [debate simulation](/students/simulations/) is one possible way to practise
challenging an interpretation; assess the evidence and reasoning students use.

In **law and medicine**, focus on currency and jurisdiction. AI models may confidently apply outdated guidelines, the wrong jurisdiction's law, or superseded clinical protocols. These are exactly the domains where confident error carries the highest practical risk, making AI literacy a professional safety issue, not just an academic one.

In **the social sciences**, focus on framing and absent perspectives. Economic models embedded in AI responses often reflect particular ideological assumptions; ask students to name the assumptions and consider alternatives. Policy analyses frequently omit distributional effects; ask students to identify who is missing from the picture.

## Integrating Evaluation into Normal Practice

The activities above work best when they are not isolated units on "AI literacy" but woven into normal course routines. A few practical approaches:

- Provide a shared, checked AI output as preparatory work, then ask students
  which claims are supported, missing, or wrong. If live interaction serves the
  outcome, provide approved access and an equivalent account-free activity.
- Build AI output evaluation into your existing source evaluation criteria. Many courses already ask students to assess the reliability of sources; extending that framework to AI outputs is a natural and low-overhead addition.
- When AI responses happen to be very good, say so explicitly. Part of critical evaluation is recognising quality, not just failure. Students should learn to distinguish between an AI response that is a useful starting point and one that is actively misleading.
- Encourage students to [prompt more carefully](/prompting/) as part of evaluation — a poorly constructed question often produces a poor response, and improving the prompt is itself a diagnostic act.

## Make evaluation assessable

Ask students to submit a short claim-check record and a justified revision:

| Claim or feature | Source and exact location | Judgment | Revision or next check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Quote or locate the relevant part | Identify a passage, dataset, calculation, or other evidence | Supported, contradicted, unsupported, or unresolved | Explain what should change and why |

Judge the quality of source selection, the match between evidence and claim,
recognition of uncertainty, and the reason for the revision. Do not grade the
number of faults found: a supported claim should be retained. Include a fresh
output later to check whether students can transfer the method.

## Example Prompt: AI Fact-Check Exercise

The prompt below can help prepare a fact-checking artefact. Generate it in an
approved environment, then inspect every claim and reference before class. Keep
a private answer key recording what is accurate, unsupported, misleading, or
missing. Select an output with useful issues; do not rely on a live model to make
an appropriate mistake during the activity.

<Callout>
Generate a 300-word explanation of [topic from the course] for a first-year
undergraduate. Use only the supplied source pack.

- A definition of the core concept
- Two or three specific examples
- Three claim-to-source citations using the identifiers in the source pack

Present uncertainty where the sources disagree. Do not invent a reference or a
claim that the source pack does not support.

[paste approved source pack]
</Callout>

After checking and selecting the artefact, ask students to:

1. Open the cited sources and check whether each cited passage supports the claim.
2. Find one factual claim that can be checked against a primary source and check it.
3. Identify one thing that a strong explanation of this topic would include that this response does not.
4. Write two sentences assessing how useful this response would be as a starting point for an essay on this topic — and why.

The final question matters: it asks students to make a judgment, not just a critique, which is the disposition you ultimately want to cultivate.
